On the day

Day of

One screen, held by whoever is running the table. Everything on it is one press.

Checking in

People arrive at base, get an apple crate and a jar, and go out. The screen shows the hour that is running, who is expected, and who has already gone.

Two shifts back to back at one shop are shown as one row, because that is what they are to the person doing them — they do not come back to the table in between. One press covers the whole stretch.

Handing the jar over is the moment somebody goes out, so it sets their shift at the same time. There is no second button to forget at the busiest point of the evening.

Somebody can take several jars. A jar that has gone out by mistake — wrong number, wrong person, never actually left — can be taken straight back, and the person only returns to "at base" if that was their last one.

Counting them back in

When a jar comes back it is counted, and that is the only place a figure is typed. The screen never asks where the jar went or who had it, because both were recorded when it was handed over. Whoever is counting at nine at night does not have to remember.

A jar can go out again afterwards. Over a Saturday the same jar might make three trips, and each is recorded separately.

Money that never went through a jar

Apples sold by the bushel, a donation handed straight to an organizer, a card tap away from the counting table. All of it is money raised at a shop, and all of it can be recorded against that shop with a note saying what it was. Refusing to record it because there was no jar is exactly how a total ends up unaccountable.

Labels and scanning

Every jar gets a printed label with its number and a code. Print a sheet before the event — or just the three whose labels peeled off, which is as ordinary a job as labelling forty new tins.

No date on the label. The same tin goes out on any day of any year — the app pairs the number with the day being worked, which is what makes it unique.

Scan a jar
Point at the label on the jar

Issuing a jar and counting one back both accept a scan instead of a typed number. At a table in the dark with cold hands and a queue behind you, that is the difference between a jar being recorded and a jar being remembered later.

Numbers can always be typed instead. A camera that will not focus, a label under a smear of apple juice, a borrowed phone that refuses permission — none of those should stop the evening.

The scanner is for organizers. A volunteer never needs it: their phone's own camera opens their link.

Swaps and no-shows

Two people can be swapped between their shifts in one action, so the schedule and the jars never disagree about who was where. Somebody who does not turn up can be marked as such, and unmarked again — including after their shift has finished, which is usually when you find out.

Marking a child as a no-show is a claim a parent may well ask about later, so it is recorded like the money is: who said so, when, and what it said before.
