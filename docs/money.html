Per hour, not just per shop

A shop that raised eight hundred dollars over eight hours is not doing as well as one that raised six hundred in three. Takings per hour is what tells you where to put people next year, and it needs the hours actually worked — which the app knows, because it knows who was checked in and for how long.

One jar often covers a run of consecutive shifts at one shop. Its takings are credited across all of them, rather than all landing on the hour it went out. Otherwise a Friday evening reads as a spectacular first hour followed by three that earned nothing.

There is only one set of figures

Every jar is counted once, so the cash and card split falls out of how each one was counted. Nothing is totalled by hand a second time.

That is deliberate. A second set of typed totals is what lets a set of books sit eighty-six dollars apart for a year without anybody noticing — both columns look right, and neither is checkable against the other.

The only figures typed in are the ones no jar can know: apples sold by the bushel, and what actually reached the bank. The deposit is there so the total has something outside itself to be checked against.

Comparing against last year

Each shop's page shows what it raised this year beside the year before, so a shop quietly falling off is visible before somebody has already committed six people to it. Only the years being compared are loaded, rather than every year the group has ever run.

Corrections

An amount is typed once, at a table, by whoever is holding the jar — and a correction at nine o'clock looks exactly like the original entry. So every change to a counted amount is recorded with who made it and what it said before, and the same goes for money moved between shops. Moving four hundred dollars from one shop to another changes which one looks worth staffing next year.
