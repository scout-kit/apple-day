What Apple Day is

Youth stand outside shops on a Friday evening and a Saturday, hand out apples, and collect donations in numbered jars. Somebody has to decide who stands where, hand out the jars, count them back in, and work out at the end of it what the weekend raised and which shops were worth the hours.

That is usually a spreadsheet. Swapping two people means retyping cells in two places. A shop spelled slightly differently on the Friday sheet and the Saturday sheet becomes two shops, each with half the takings. Money gets totalled by hand in one column and again in another, and by midnight the two disagree by eighty-odd dollars that nobody can find.

This replaces that. Nothing is joined up by matching names against each other, every jar is counted exactly once, and the arithmetic is checked against a real year's results before it ever runs on a new one.

Two shifts back to back at one shop are one row. The person doing them does not come back to the table in between, and does not want checking in twice.

Two decisions worth naming

Nobody is told where they are going until they arrive

A volunteer's link shows their times straight away, but names the shop only once an organizer has checked them in at base. Everybody reports to base first anyway, and a link that gets forwarded around should not say where a named child will be standing for the next two hours. Reminder emails carry the times and never the places.

A jar that is still out is not a jar that came back empty

Until a jar is counted, it has no amount — which is different from having an amount of nothing. Treating the two the same drags a shop's takings-per-hour down while the day is still running, and hides how much is still out on the street. Jars that have not come back are listed separately so somebody can go and find them.
